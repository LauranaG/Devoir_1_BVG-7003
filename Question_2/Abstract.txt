**Abstract:**

The number of retracted scientific publications has risen in recent years, prompting questions about the underlying causes of this trend. To explore this, we analyzed 2,047 retracted articles, focusing on trends in the time interval between publication and retraction (time-to-retraction). The average time-to-retraction across all articles was 32.91 months. However, articles published before 2002 took longer to retract compared to those published after 2002.

To assess the role of increased scrutiny, we examined the relationship between time-to-retraction and journal impact factor (IF). While high-IF journals tended to retract articles more quickly, this factor explained only 1% of the variance. Therefore, while greater scrutiny in prominent journals may play a role, it does not fully explain the increase in retractions.

Retractions for rare offenses have become more frequent. The first recorded retraction for plagiarism occurred in 1979, with plagiarism now accounting for 9.4% of all retractions. Additionally, the study found that retractions are more likely to occur earlier, with a decline for articles published since 2000.

Another factor driving the rise in retractions is the behavior of authors with a single retraction, who have become more prominent. From 1993 to 2012, 63.1% of retracted papers were written by authors with only one retraction, compared to 46% from 1972 to 1992. This shift suggests that retractions are now more broadly distributed rather than being concentrated among a few offenders.

In conclusion, the rise in retractions reflects changes in the behavior of both authors and institutions.
